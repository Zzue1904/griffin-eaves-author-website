Chapter One

Foreword

_______________________

Chaos is like a little bug with all the loudest colours you can think of tattooed on his back, blaring, "Don't eat me! I'm poisonous!" And so you think, Well, alright then, and you go back to reading your book or watching your show and after five minutes of perfectly horrific boredom you lean over headfirst and devour him whole. And as the toxins course through your veins and you rock back and forth, head in hands, agonised for a solid two or three business days, you think, I'll never do that again, and then you will. And then you will. And then you will. This diary was originally a digital series of blog posts updated every now-and-again from November 5th, 2023, to January 7th, 2025. It has been adapted and re-edited as a sort of messy, underloved novella—or perhaps something like a transcript of a lunatic's inane ramblings taken by a stealthy undercover journalist with a lot of spare time and nothing better to do.

It covers over a year of my time spent in rural, then metropolitan Japan as I worked at a sake brewery, mountaintop hotel, tour company, and a nice old lady's house as her resident fridge raider and snack eater. It covers my time going insane in the lonely dead of winter, uncovering ancient graffiti in run-down hotels, sleeping in my car, and starving. It also covers some of the most important lessons I have ever learned and experiences I have ever had. As is proper for a work of such spiritual magnitude and significance, mine is a serious, sophisticated book that retains an air of elegance and refinement from the first words to the last acknowledgements.

Living in a farmhouse with family in Iiyama. Not employed.

Nov 5, 2023 "Way to Fall"

No matter where I am in the world, I always feel the most comfortable right here on the toilet. Travelling can be extremely stressful, but it's the actual arriving that unnerves me the most. Once you step through the gate at the airport, you're stuck on a set path. You become a simple mechanism in an overly long linear track that takes you through the many pointless conventions of airport customs, boarding passes, and–perhaps most arbitrarily–security. You wouldn't believe how many ceramic knives I snuck past those jobbers. This time I hit my record.

My point is that until I don't have a gate to find, a connecting flight to board, or a bag to retrieve from the carousel–until I am free–I'm never at my most stressed-out. "Welp," I think, "it's out of my hands. I have to sit here and watch Mulan eighteen times next to this extremely elbowy old man until I land." When you're free there is no such comforting thought. When I arrived here in Iiyama via the shinkansen station, it was terribly dark and starless. And it was terribly cold. And I was terribly free.

If someone asked me if I was scared of the dark I'd say no because I am a liar. I can sleep in a dark room and I can close my eyes without freaking out but I think that's because darkness is only really dark when it has volume. Volume. It's a very round word for a very spherical, cornerless feeling. In that way, a big empty Japanese darkness is the perfect playmate for the newfound sensation of being truly aimless and frighteningly capable of doing many, many things wrong. That's not a sensation that comes to me at an airport. That sensation is just called boredom.

Bathrooms are a lot like airports. That's kind of my point. You can't leave until you've done what you came to do. You can't really mess it up. There is no real unpredictability. Until I stand up–to use the present as an example–I won't get anything out of worrying about what to do next or how to maximise my time or whatever else. I can just be in this small un-mysterious room for a while and it's not even my fault. You might be thinking: "Damn, this dude sounds stressed out!" and you'd be dead wrong. You and my smartwatch. I almost love the feeling of uncertainty, the volume of it. Life has volume, and so does fresh air, or the future. All good things.

I now live in a refurbished farmhouse on the frayed old edge of Iiyama town in northern Nagano Prefecture. I live here with my cousin and his wife who called me out of the blue to invite me to work at a sake brewery two blocks away from their house. I arrived here in the darkness of the night. Little pinhole lights seared the black like the focused rays of a magnifying glass. They were far, far away, but until I saw the mist melt over the mountains today, and the glades of green and vastness of valley unfurl unendingly outside this morning, I could not have fathomed how large this place was—how large the world. It's like a damn postcard. I tied some rope around a giant pile of straw with some farmers and sanded down a table. I sat in the sunshine and the wind for hours. I caught 80+ stink bugs in a plastic bottle because it's more ethical to trap them in a portable mass grave and watch them suffocate on the fumes of a mountain of beetle corpses than it is to squish 'em. I drank tea made of toothpicks, ate a single deep-fried grape, hit some of the notes in "Don't Stop Me Now" at karaoke, did some foggy jogging, and spoke a lot of Japanese. This is all to say that things, which seemed to have stopped for a time in my previous life, are now going again.

No idea how to flush this thing.
