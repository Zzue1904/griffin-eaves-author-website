SINGLE POINT

Chapter One

II.

The wind chilled, but blew gently. It had gained but pitiful strength since the complete absence of gales at all nearer to the gate of Ordania, and it had taken a week’s travel to see it blow even that weakly. Destro could not be brought by chill or roaring storm to look another moment at the whitened trees, the endless road before him. A straighter labyrinth there never existed, for it felt to the pair at the dawn of the seventh day like there had been no inches crawled to their destination. It was unthinkable that the forest for all its cold could ever give way to what they knew as a temperate desert, upon which stood the fortress of their objective. Instead, Destro looked to his bag of supplies, and picked at the spoiling contents, lamenting only that he had not eaten it all and died on a good meal rather than starving piece by piece on rotting fruit and scraps of meat. Alto, as he had done each morning since his meeting with Seere a week past, stared out beyond the treeline and into the woods ever beside the trail, staring, searching intently for something. Destro paid little attention; he had long run dry of ways to insult his companion for something that even he considered only mildly idiotic. Yet, Destro could not bear to look at his remaining food, and he could not bear to look again at the blankness of space and earth around him, and he could not bear to look one more moment at the weakling staring off, he who would care for nothing but faith if his belly was empty. And it assuredly was. If there was another regret in Destro, it was that most of his companion’s chosen supplies were non-perishable, and he still rarely ate at them.

“I want more,” Destro said. He stood, and took from his possessions his sword and a long strand of cord, which he set to work tying into a noose. Alto stood up. It would have taken far less to rouse him from his close attention to the deeper trees. “We’re going hunting.”

“Hunting?” Alto relaxed, barely. “There is nothing here, everywhere we have seen.”

“Oh no, Alto, packer of long-lasting supplies. Oh no.”

Destro’s intonation was ominous, but his posture was determined.

He beckoned Alto to follow him as he stepped off the path.

“Destro! None wander from the path, and neither can we. Even you won’t suffer death for some imagined thing.”

“Is that not sacrifice? What you describe? Someday you’ll put yourself to the spear for faith, inflated on imaginary courage and deflated for some ‘imagined thing.’” Destro mimicked Alto, not poorly, in those last words. “And besides, I saw a deer not a night before now.”

“You sicken me.”

“What?” Destro was shocked, and set quickly to wondering if he had really deserved the insult, though he shortly arrived at the affirmative. His laughter was partly nervous, but well trained and, to an untrained eye, unhurt.

“All you know is to lie, Destro. To me, to the pontiff, and to God. The road has been long, but I will not be your entertainment.” Alto sat back down and stared into the treeline opposite to Destro.

“You need not worry about that, Alto. You would not fit that role for all your trying, you… blank, boring, nothing-man. Oh, how in greater company the road would be short, and the nights would be long and drunken. Haha!” Destro threw his hands into the air.

“If you had the company of another you, you’d starve twice,” Alto said. There was no humour in his comment, nor was there compassion. Destro scoffed at this, and turned away to wander into the frozen woods. Only when he was nearly out of sight in between the thick trunks of the trees did Alto call out again. “Destro!” he cried. “Enough! That way is only death, and you will lose the road.” He received no response, and could no longer see his companion. “I can help you set a trap! If it could be here, on the path!” There was only quiet in the air; no footsteps of Destro’s could be heard, and no snide response. Alto took two lonely steps towards the edge of the path. Then, from behind one of the nearer trees to the road, a green-hooded head poked out slowly.

“The sound of courage is a beautiful novelty, when only it comes from you my friend. And it seems fresher and newer each time, before it is scoured by fear. I will make use of the bolder you while he exists. You will leap upon the back of the beast while I strangle it with the noose,” Destro said, fully emerged from behind the tree now.

“What sort of hunting is that?” Alto’s worry melted into annoyance.

“When we are by my methods led to the smell of braising, the rush of victory, it shall be a sort you don’t regret, Alto the bolder.” Destro had gone to Alto’s supplies and nearly begun to look through them. Even before Alto called out to him, however, he hesitated.

“Hey!” Alto wasn’t sure what to do, or what to ask. He had promised to help, but the method that Destro sought to employ seemed a fragile, incomplete notion. Destro left Alto’s things and searched his own, taking some of the better fruits and placing them upon the path. He swept the firewood away and placed the bedrolls and bags of supplies beside the path, and nearly out of sight. Destro swept some snow from his hands, and stood to admire that the path was empty, save for a pile of food in its center.

“There is a deer, Alto. I saw it not long ago. It pranced across the path while we slept. I did say that between the two of us I would be the watcher of things, did I not? Observation is an important skill at all times, so I will be the one to climb the tree, and you will be the one to wait for my signal. Boost me up,” Destro said. Alto begrudgingly helped Destro get a foothold on the tree most nearby to the path, and watched astounded as the man betrayed all his age and apparent frailty, clambering to the lowest branch and clinging to it with few problems. The noose cord he held tightly in his hand, as Alto sat against the side of the tree. “Not there! Hide where I point with my sword now, behind those two, between them. When the noose is tight, spring upon it! Haha! Even in failure we shall have some entertainment, and it will be you after all.”

“How long will we wait?” Alto asked, sure of the plan’s failure, knowing now how Destro planned to proceed, and guessing that the man would gamble it all, if the deer ever came, on a throw of a noose from a very uncomfortable position in a tree, against whose frozen whiteness he stood out like a rotten green apple.

“How long is a string?” “You’ve lost your mind.” “As long as you cut it.”

The two were silent, and waiting. Alto looked to the sky, lamenting the distance he might have made if only his companion had sense. The clouds mixed and swirled, grey against the gold of the sky. And it was gold, that vault of heaven ever hanging gently over desolate earth and frozen trees. It was gold every day, a crowning sky for an unworthy world. Alto rarely wondered anymore at the properties of other lands, beyond the mountainous borders of his home. Yet he hoped, were they better or worse, that they should have that same gilded sky above for him to see always, and after the reaching of distant lands. Destro looked at a pile of rotting fruit. His observations were limited to sight, alas, for a deer could not break the silent curse of the woods, only he. He hoped that the rustling of his companion was a threat unknown to creatures here, who might have learned to ignore such feelings as sound having never used them. Only, if creatures had learned to train their sight in absence of sound here, it would be best to hope the deer, incoming, could not look up at him, for he fidgeted on his uncomfortable perch, noose in hand.

An hour passed before Destro noticed something dark out of the corner of his eye, and feared that Alto had given up and moved. Yet, there was no humanity to its movements, and its graceful wandering soon registered as that innocent canter of a four-legged beast. A deer, clad in thick, black fur, with horns short and round, wandered from the white of the forest like an ink-spot dripping down an empty page. And what’s more, it soon stood right before the food on the path, and, cursed by silence, would emit no sound as it began to eat. Joy swelled in Destro, and met with the anxiety that he might miss, though he readied the rope anyways, and prepared to throw the opening around the creature’s neck like a lasso. Below, Alto studied the movements of his companion, who he could see had changed in temper drastically, now that the beast had emerged. Alto, knowing it may be his time to strike, began to stand up. Yet, his foot did slip from a rock, and click some broken splinter below him. Alto froze, and turned his head upwards, expecting to see that the deer had fled. Though, a turn more strange had occurred at his sound, and as his eye wandered towards the path, it met and locked intensely with the rectangular pupils of a staring, black deer. Neither moved, and atop the tree Destro thought only of the throw, and how to perfect it knowing the deer might, any moment, recognize the danger Alto posed with his hand already on his sword. Alto’s thoughts, however, were not on any danger he could bring on his own, but that which was already there, increasing in apparent intensity the longer he stared at the beast, and the thicker the coat of black hair grew, and the more widened and sideways its muzzle became, and the further rows of sharpened, gnarled teeth could be seen within its gaping red smile like rocks of pearl amidst a bloody maelstrom.

Destro understood. If he had wondered at what adaptations the creatures of the wood may have undergone to protect themselves before, he wondered no longer, regretting only that he had not predicted such danger, in what might have once been easy prey. He threw the noose without thinking of the difficulty, and when it fell perfectly around the creature’s head, he made no considerations of victory, only preparations to pull hard and stay pulling. Contrary to any quiet curses, the beast screamed, and its cries ravaged that heaven-kept silence entirely. No wind or gale could permeate the air as such a scream did easily; it was not deer-like completely and indeed it held certain timbres of other forest creatures hidden within its sound. It clicked like a squirrel, and hooted like an owl, though each creature it mimicked or had once consumed emerged in sound like the manifestation of all their previous agony, fear and hatred. Destro heaved on the rope and as he did, the branch he lay upon, face down, sank as the beast pulled against him. Destro roared, and his commands were without fashion. To Alto they were the familiar barks of his commanders, and though he was afraid—dreadfully—he awoke from his paralysis at those orders, where he might have submitted to death without. As he leapt, sword in hand, his prior hesitation was his chagrin, and his action its own reward as he plunged his blade forth. Yet the beast, in all its thrashing, knocked the metal away with its head, and sent a forward kick into Alto’s chest, knocking him away.

The beast raised upwards, hardly burdened by Destro’s death-grip upon the rope, stamping down freely upon Alto, who thrashed to avoid the heavy stomping of its hardened hooves. Alto caught many blows, whether glancing or full, and his pain soon ate at his will to reach for his sword. The maw of the creature was agonised and imbibed of strangled rage. Without Destro to pull the rope, its blows might have cracked the earth. Destro stood upon the branch now, raising himself up to his fullest height and pressing down on the branch with straightened legs as he held the end of the noose as high as he could. Still the creature raged, brought up on winning desperate fights for scarce survival.

Against such natural brutality, it was natural that men should fall. Fall. Destro had an idea, then. He leapt from the branch, opposite to the side the noose hung tightly, falling with the rope in his hands and betraying steadily applied force for a risky, yet instant snap of strength. He would not hit the earth; his descent was stopped instantly by the tautness of the rope, wrapped over the branch and affixed still to the raging creature’s neck. It was as a reverse hanging, where the pulling force was the fall of the executioner, not the executed. And the beast was pulled upwards to stand on its hind legs, its neck snapping hard and its cries yelping, pained. Yet it thrashed still, lived still, until Alto, who reached across the path to regain his sword, flung his arm across his body and sent sailing that sharpened blade into the creature’s neck, as though it were a spear. And the sword, splitting flesh and bone alike, as well severed the noose and freed the creature’s bleating body to fall and splatter on the ground. Destro, his rope tied to nothing, then fell a short distance onto his back. The two sat, inviting the welcome silence to resume its flowing through the cold woods.

Eventually, Destro stood and retrieved a small knife from his pocket. Without ceremony, he wandered towards the corpse of the deer and set about skinning it, pausing momentarily to consider the protrusions in its belly. He gave a glance to Alto over his shoulder, who continued to gasp for air even minutes after the deed was done, clutching blunt injuries on his arms and legs. Destro turned away without a word, and sank his knife into the belly to investigate. It was then, after the blade was pulled across and the entrails were spilt, that Destro calmly placed his knife on the ground, and walked away from the corpse towards his belongings beside the path. He brought Alto his things and began to wander down the road, abandoning the body to the cold. Alto, despite his pains, was infinitely curious at this, considering the work the both of them had put into slaying the creature, until he stood, shaking off his wounds as minor and already slightened by time, and went to examine the deer himself. Alto’s eye widened as he looked inside, and he was inspired by the sight to vomit upon the legs of the carcass as he tried to turn fully away. Soon, he was after Destro on the path, for the belly of the thing was made entirely of smaller woodland creatures, all melted together into brutal crimson entrails of independents fused, all inedible for their appearance alone.
