GOODNIGHT EARTH

Chapter One

[thigh bone]

“I just thought of a joke,” she said.

The walls were dark and terrible, amassed of a pure sheen of textureless pitch that converted what should have been an entirely claustrophobic conveyor room into a spacious, false, and eternal starless night. Even distance could not be told, and it could not be said where the hissing, worming pipe-nests slithered discreetly over and under one another, nor where the oil buboes, sloshing gently from the distant, shuddering machinations of droning equipment, sagged limp. And though it could not be said where and in what shape those jet walls stood, there was no mistaking that they curled in at their corners, rounding and wrapping that unseen place in a sphere. Perhaps it was the way the air moved cyclically, endlessly that betrayed this shape, and yet even air could scarcely be felt, for feeling the room devoured as easily as sight. In another bleak mile of factory floor, there were rooms where drill-bores rattled their stunted diesel shrieks and not a shred of quiet could be found. Here, things were quiet indeed, and the air merely hummed.

“A what?” said the other.

“A joke. It’s funny. Hm. No. Let me find a better page.”

There was a raised conveyor belt built into the centre of the room, feeding from one narrow pore and across into another, though it was still, for now. Though it was lined with softly glowing white bulbs, the dimness of their light could make no further shadows upon the already lightless wall. What could be seen from that light was the conveyor itself - which was then empty - and its two, looming hosts.

She was a gnarled, thin tube of bumpy rubber that stretched outwards from the dark, creaking and snapping as it turned, as if a neck eternally breaking. And upon the end of that tube there was a head - or what might be mistaken for one in abject darkness. It was a square, metal skull whose parts had once been haphazardly welded together, leaving visible dents, grooves, and seams all scarcely illumed by the bulbs. This was all of her. On the back of her head, the engraving read: DEVELOPS INTRADEPENDENCIES.

“This is a killer book, Shakes. You can read it when I’m done. Here’s a good page. Hmm. ‘For a healthier mind, consider limiting your daily intake of red meat.’ That’s so fascinating.”

“Is that a good joke?” The other was a tube. And a skull. And upon his skull was an engraving that read: SHAKES WHEN SORTING.

“No Shakes, that isn’t a joke at all. I told you I was finding another page. My book has plenty of good stuff in it, and not just jokes but serious bits too. Heehee.”

“Hey Intra.”

“Yes?”

“Fresh ones coming in.” The conveyor had begun to move with a shudder, and slick new metres of it came rolling in and out of the room from where it was fed in and let out. Still, nothing was upon it. That would come later. As “Shakes” bent his long neck down with a snap, more of the white bulbs lit his face. Just like her, his head was bare and metal, his eyes empty, his mouth wide and sharp. It was solid, and could show no expression, no matter what was said or felt.

“No way! You take care of it. I just got to the good part,” said the one called Intra.

“You’re just gonna watch?” Shakes panicked.

“No Shakes. I’m not going to watch, because then I wouldn’t be able to read my book. Then there wouldn’t be much point in not helping, would there? Think before you speak, please.” Intra had already turned aside. Despite the pair’s appearance, their voices were hardly as hollow as their forms. Shakes always spoke with a certain level of audible paranoia, and Intra with no lack of bored righteousness. Though, it is true, she had gotten much worse when she had started talking about this book of hers. Nevertheless, there was a metal tinge to their voices, as though they spoke from out the mouths of deep iron caves. The conveyor continued, and grew loud and churning. An alarm blared suddenly, in one uncomfortably long chord. One could hear the hissing of steam, though not see it. Shakes looked back and forth nervously, and Intra paid no mind.

“‘If you turn to page eighty-six, you can see the completed puzzle,’” Intra recited. “Oh! Shakes! Shakes!” “Me?” Shakes turned. “Yes, you, you! It says right here. You have to turn to page eighty-six. You can see the completed puzzle!” “The what?” “The puzzle. It’s completed. Hm. Well that’s odd. How come only ‘you’ can see it? I’d like to see it also.” Intra’s head swung slowly back and forth in wide arcs. “I dunno. It’s your book.”

“Tch. Yeah, it is my book. So why the hell are you in it? Come to think of it… yeah, everything in here is about you, Shakes. What a load. ‘You should get twelve hours of sleep,’ ‘you will live to see manmade…’ This is rubbish. Ooh, wait wait, here’s a good one: ‘helping people makes you feel good.’ Damn, I’d certainly like to feel good.”

“I could use some help with the bits when they come down the conveyor.”

“No Shakes, it says helping people makes you feel good, not me. I don’t get anything for helping. It’s a raw deal.”

“Why just me?” Shakes turned towards the rumbling sound of machinery, which steadily rose to a muffled clamour beyond the wall. Soon the conveyor would bring them in.

“I don’t know. Dumbass book.” “What does that mean?” “It’s a bad thing. You’re supposed to say it when you’re mad.” “I am?” “Everyone is. It’s a really bad thing, haha.”

Rhythmic clanks and whirring brought the machinery to an unceasing and infuriatingly loud tone. From the pores in the wall, small objects could be seen travelling the stained conveyor and coming into the light one by one. Shakes got to work. Intra did nothing. With his mouth, he pierced the newly arrived contents of the conveyor as they slowly passed by. The belt moved slowly; it could take minutes for a piece coming from one pore to reach the further end of the room and disappear into the other one, and this usually afforded the pair much time to get the job done. Yet Shakes worked alone, and his motions were scrambled and confused, pushed beyond their regular speed by the absence of his usual assistant. As he gripped the items, placing them in black-bagged buckets beside the conveyor, Shakes started shaking.

“Renal artery, ten centimetres. Talus, two grams. Amygdala, entire,” he said warily. Each item he placed in a different bag, though none were marked. “Aw nice, that’s rare. Well done Shakes.” Intra stretched her jaw. “Intra, they’re gonna get past me. I’m too slow.” “Hmm. Well you could stop shaking so bad. The whole line is quaking.” “That’s not funny.” “You’re like an earthquake in an earthworm. Haha.” “I can’t help it.” “Yeah, well it’s written right on you. It’s like a testament.”

Intra coiled around and re-read the engraving on Shakes’ head, with a gesture clearly over-practiced.

“Yours isn’t so good either.” Shakes turned away from the conveyor, and in so doing nearly let a red strip of matter slide right by and into the wall, where it would vanish forever, unsorted. “Intradependencies are wicked. I’ll develop them all day.” “What is an intradependency?” “I know what it is,” Intra said, looking away. “Hey, you almost missed one.”

The objects were escaping Shakes, running down the length of the conveyor in thick enough groups that his large metal head lacked the dexterity to separate them quick enough, or to grab only one at a time and sort each to his best ability. Intra stared blankly at him as he flailed and snapped.

“I’m sorry Shakes, but think about all the dope stuff I’m gonna learn. You should just ignore me.” “How do you figure that?” Shakes vibrated constantly, rattling from stem to stern. “Well, I… um… well, it’s in the book. I’ve just put it in.” Intra grinned smugly, even though she couldn’t. Whenever an item passed all the way down the conveyor and slipped through the exit pore, a loud alarm blared. Before Shakes spoke again, it blared three more times. “Can I write something in the book?” he asked. “Ha, no. It would be too new, anyway. That’s no good.”

Intra was utterly enraptured by herself, and didn’t even notice when Shakes abandoned the conveyor and swung his skull right in front of her.

The alarm blared almost constantly, now. Every item that passed down the slick, black, sliding panels was vanishing into nothing, absorbing into the grim night-wall. Failure after failure they went, and one could hear in the growing rumble of distant machines that the very factory itself felt this neglect of duty. When Intra noticed at last, she panicked, lunging forward and headbutting Shakes out of the way. She was much faster at sorting the items than Shakes ever was, though half as willing to ever actually do it. Nonetheless, the ominous lurching sounds beyond the walls had compelled her, frightened her. It was as if the walls were growing to burst and rend their solitude away, bringing all the outside inwards; it was as if each blare of the alarm brought the hammer ever downward.

“Cheap trick, Shakes,” she said once the task was in hand again, and both were working. As the bucket bags were filled, the darkened trappings of the floor went into action. As each bucket came to a certain weight the floor would give way to it, sending it plummeting into a further void.

“T1, entire. Pineal, half-gram.” Shakes concentrated. “It’s wrong to ignore people. I don’t ignore you. I’m your friend.” The conveyor stopped, and the pair moved what remained at their own leisure. A humming quiet returned to the room, which was made more monstrously large without the awful grinding of intense machines to fill the gaps. Shakes and Intra looked right at each other, and as their deep, skeletal eye sockets grew parallel, they were joined like an endless, unbroken tunnel. They did not touch; there was a millimetre of space between their heads as they did so. “I’m sorry,” Shakes said. He could hear her inflated self-importance like the curling of metal lips. She spoke. “Sometimes I think that all these little bits and bobs we sort through are like little people, and I am their king. They only really matter in that context because I believe they do, so in a way I'm completely responsible for the life I pretend they have, you know?”

“What? They’re just bits.” Shakes moved away. “No Shakes. I’m like a God.”
