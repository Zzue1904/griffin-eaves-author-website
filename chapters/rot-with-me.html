ROT WITH ME

Chapter One

1.1: Warren

I am a reasonable man. I don’t like to see my own blood. I like having time. I like spending time in a way I think is wise, and later thinking back and knowing I did everything I could. I like reading and writing, and in fact it is my passion to see the world and put it to the page, and to see my brains drip from the edge of the pen to make my memories live forever. I don’t like it when I lose a button on my coat as I had that day on Celestine St., but I must admit the chaos of a partially buttonless coat excites me. Chaos often excites me. Sometimes I think that I could see the whole world burn to cinders and that by God I could wade through the ashes waist deep and be calm and peaceful so long as it wasn’t my fault. So long as I had nothing to do with it. So long as I was just an innocent man who nobody knew had committed any crime at all, save for misplacing that button. I don’t like to see blood. But it could spill and spill and, if it wasn’t my fault, I’d be okay. I’d be okay.

What I first noticed was the stink. Old boards and dust don’t smell like that house did. A ceiling seething dust, mouse bites in the legs of a table and two chairs, mould between tiles of a kitchen floor—none of those things smell like that. I scanned over them on walking in, and thought how claustrophobic the inside of a house, however run-down, that looked so large, so full from the street, could be. It smelled like meat. And darkness makes everything smell worse.

“Go on, sit down,” came a voice from the other side of the room, just right of the stairs leading to the second floor. There was a lamp glowing on the lower landing. It was the only source of light in the place besides the sunset crawling through thin, careless seams in the boards of the exterior walls. How dark I thought it would be when the sun went out, and how strange the only lamp should be placed there on the staircase where its rays were constrained by the stairwell walls, hardly illuminating anything on the ground floor. I could barely see him. For the softness that he used for his “Sit down,” I might not have known he was even there, limp in an armchair against the furthest wall, facing the door. He was so still as to vanish, and his skin was dark enough to seem to grow into the deep fabric of the chair. He was grafted into that chair, that darkness, that smell of meat. I thought to leave. But he was my new neighbour, after all.

The way he had pronounced “Sit down” was more like “Siddown,” and in fact most of his sentences sort of melted together into efficient, melodical strings. Even in the darkness I saw he was rather gaunt, and he would breathe long and tired after each sentence. It was like watching moss breathe. I sat down in the kitchen, which was off to the right of the door I had walked through and closed. He did not turn his head to watch me creak across the floor. Even after my eyes had begun to adjust, I still couldn’t make out the features of his face, and I wondered if he hadn’t always been watching that empty chair, knowing I would come. That certainly fit in with something else I had noticed.

“Do people usually leave their doors unlocked here?” I asked, swiping dust from the table with a finger. “No. Folks don’t say ‘Come in’ too often neither,” he said. “We’ll make good neighbours then,” I said. “Actually I’m just a few doors down. White paint. Single floor. I’m Warren.” “Just Warren?” “Yeah.” “Happy to meet you,” he said joylessly. I thought, regardless, that there was some sincerity to this. There was sincerity to everything he said. “Andy,” he finished. “Like I said at the door, this won’t take long. I don’t want to bother you,” I went on. “None taken.” “I’m a disease doc- ah, studier. I study disease.” “Good timing, Warren,” he said.

I rubbed my index finger and thumb together until the slickness of my sweat gave way to an even more uncomfortable friction. It was hot, humid, and imperfect weather for a coat, fully-buttoned or not. I said nothing for a few seconds. They felt eternal. I took the time to notice something else about the room, about the house. There was a consistent creaking coming from upstairs somewhere. Every fifteen-or-so seconds it would sound, with some discrepancy. It was alive, that sound, like a pained animal. I feared it. It gripped me how miserable the scene was, and I slumped. I think Andy caught that moment, saw the hesitation in the gentle grinding of my teeth. The kitchen, like the rest of the home, was lightless save for the vestige rays of the lamp on the stair. Yet I had sat next to one of those seams in the wood, and the last of the sunlight had started coming through at just the angle to write a golden path across my face. I felt it unfair that he, hidden, could freely look over the details of my face as I spoke, and only by my chance encounter with the sunbeam. When I asked my next question I was ready to act on this injustice. I leaned in, elbows on the table, and immersed myself in darkness again. And we two were unseen.

“Do you know anyone suffering from yellow fever?” I asked. “Yes.” “Do you have any theory how they got it?” “Breathin’.” “Can you describe the symptoms?” “Dyin’.”

I didn’t even shift. I knew I needed to be as stoic as he was for some reason, all wrapped in darkness, stuck into that chair. I felt I needed to push on without pause or I would never get through. But damn, did I feel that word. Damn, did I feel the pain there and keep pushing like I didn’t care at all.

“How.” I said. I spoke like I held the reigns of an interrogation. Maybe I thought that would speed things along. It was pitifully sad there. It smelled like meat. I just wanted to get out, to jot my notes and start my terrible work at home, and to lock my damn door.

But I didn’t have control. I realised this when Andy rose like a root stretching to snap, and I saw his arms and legs lift from the chair like storm-swaying branches, not terrifyingly quickly but awfully, dreadfully slow. And I couldn’t move, only lean back in my chair as he approached so that my face re-entered the sunbeam, and he could see my fear while I could see nothing of him. The darkness of his corner followed him across the floor like a tide. I marvelled at the cruelty in his voice then, and how it could turn so bitter from being so weak, so given up.

“Throwin’ up. Bleedin’. Spinnin’, crawlin’, cryin’, prayin’, shakin’, spillin’ from your mouth, your back, your front. Your guts curlin’ up like a bug. Can’t see. Can’t breathe. No thinkin’,” he said. He had stepped a few times towards the kitchen, but not into the light. With each step he seemed to grow and his voice emerge from higher until I thought the darkness might have no ceiling at all, and that he was a giant with his head in a dark cloud. He went on, “Then you spit it all up, all of it,” and as he did he shrank again, voice going soft. All of a sudden he was in that chair again, in that dark, and I could see his eyes somehow, turned up, not to a stormcloud but to a ceiling after all. And everything seemed to crunch down again. “Then you die, man.”

The floor above creaked again. It struck me that Andy was looking right under the spot where it seemed the loudest. That’s where he had put his chair. For the intensity of his stare I thought he might have a peephole above him, and be watching the source of that creaking without blinking. He was totally alien, then. Not pathetic as when I came in, nor godlike as when he had stood, but mysterious. It wasn’t sinister and it wasn’t sad. The sight of him sitting there, staring up, fit together with the rest of the scene like a puzzle piece. And I realised that I could only see his eyes then, and only barely, because the scarce light had found some extra water on them through which to glisten and sparkle.

I had a notebook in my left hand and a pen in my right. The blank page had excited me before I had stepped through the door. Now it was just blank. It had been weeks by horse and cart to this trainless city of New Orleans, and instantly I wanted nothing to do with cobbled streets or neighbours or coffee or people, nothing at all. I tipped my hat, stood, and without a word made for the door. I didn’t look back until I heard from behind, as softly as he had yet spoken, “Don’t go having a daughter, mister.” It was the first time he had looked right at me.

___

My mood picked up as soon as the door was closed. All it takes is to breathe some fresh air, sometimes. Fresh air and turning away, these things are there for me. I won’t say I had enjoyed the miles of bumpy track overland from the station at Atlanta all the miserably hot way down south, though I had nothing in higher excess then than fresh air and ignorance, my two favourite hobbies. They were building a railroad from Memphis to New Orleans, I knew. They’d be ten years too late. This sickness in Louisiana was happening now. I needed to see it, now, I had thought. At its peak. And I did. That sunset of the 28th of July, 1853, presaged the worst summer for yellow fever in Louisiana’s history. The air was hot and fresh.

In fact it seemed, that night, to be fresher than I had ever breathed it. I felt alive, and I will admit that as the rotten door behind me closed, a smile wrinkled my face, almost a laugh. I was fascinated. How interesting it was! How dark! How like the tragic seat of Milton’s devil the scene, how like a descent through Dante’s circles! I was having fun again. Trains and buckboard carts do fine, of course. They’re an adventure. From a train you can say goodbye and good riddance and you’ll never hear a word in reply, if you don’t seek it. From a cart you can see the stars and worry sick at every rustling in the bushes at night. But in both cases there is only the getting there, at the very end. That’s all one needs to think about. I don’t get my chaos from a cart in the night, surrounded by nothing. But I could get it here, on a cobbled street, with neighbours and coffee and people all around, all things I had decried a moment ago inside that house. All it takes is to breathe some fresh air. Then anything can blow away, and new joys come.

Mine was that sunset, sucking its orange roots back across the length of New Orleans. Looking southwest from the east of Celestine St. I couldn’t see much further than the higher tenements some streets away. Charming homes flanked the road; most were perfectly the same, all lined up behind overgrowing grass that alone betrayed they may all hide some sadness inside, some poverty. But how the glow worked wonders on them. And as it faded, and the homes lost their lustre as the sun fell away, it was like they had been raptured by the glow, as if the pitch-black of the night did not just obscure them all but that they were gone completely, truly. This is when I realised I had been walking north along the avenue for hours. There were no lamplights in that part of the city, then. To that darkness I owed the illusion of being all alone, and for it I would have an extremely difficult walk back home.

What good is having a house covered in peeling white paint when everything goes dark? I waddled through that night like a drunk, touching and feeling every post, nearly tripping into every water trough, regretting very much about how I liked to spend my evenings. When I found that door again it was with an extreme relief. When I opened it there was only complete disappointment, the realisation that your lamps are cold inside, and that beyond the threshold is just an extension of the night until you bang your head on enough cabinets to remember where the oil is, hoping each impact jogs your memory and you remember how to fill a lamp without being able to see it.

As my fingers slipped with oil I wondered how anyone was surprised that half the city burned down every year. The solution seemed to be to have troughs and buckets outside each house. I prefer street lanterns. But then, I only had so much money to spare, coming here. No Canal St. apartment for me, no stiff, upright Garden District mansion. The plan was a slum for three productive months, a return trip, and a roaring symphony of acclaim for the premiere amateur epidemiologic work of our era.

Someone once told me that “epidemiology” was what people were going to start calling the study of disease. I found it hard to believe. We might as well have been wearing beaked masks and popping buboes, and that sort of medical ignorance didn’t change from Boston to this southern gem of the new world. Yet doesn’t the innovation of one feed on the ignorance of others? I was on the edge of the study. Me. I wanted to be cited someday, with a name I had chosen, a name I had made. That’s why I had come.

All that, I thought, would start with an old table, a musky green armchair, a teetering stool, a kitchen counter, a fireplace below a chimney, and a bookcase. Really, the kitchen counter may have just been a regular counter, but in my experience counters are in kitchens, which made the otherwise unremarkable, identical, unthresholded wooden floor in front of this counter, as it would, the kitchen. For these things and a roof over my head I was extremely lucky. Death had left many houses behind. But what I was most lucky for were the contents of the bookcase. Everything else had been stripped between the last owner and myself except a few choice books left forgotten on the shelf. Shelley, Homer, Swift—I was awestruck that they should be there, right beside me. It was a luxury.

It almost seemed wrong. This was not the place for such work. I thought I had come to the edge of the world and still I stared at some of the most familiar companions I ever knew. I had already read them, obviously, and I would do so again, with my nice green armchair facing the southern, backmost wall, which seemed massive without any fixings or decoration to spoil it besides the bookcase itself. I don’t know why I chose to face this wall rather than the sole window staring out the front of the house and into the road. Maybe I felt I had more control this way. Maybe beyond the boards I felt whatever horizon I imagined lay there in earnest. Maybe I thought I could almost see the shore of Daneland and the ships of the Geats as I read of them, or the plains of ice into which Frankenstein’s monster fled. The wooden boards of that southern wall were my canvas. I could go anywhere.

But where I imagined I went first was the house a few doors down with the man sitting rooted in that chair, all too much like mine. I went to his kitchen and let a sunbeam burn my skin. I heard the crack and creak of the boards of the second floor. I saw the man cry darkly and the lamp on the turn of the stair flicker, threatening to go out for good, to separate him from whatever terror writhed itself to sleep upstairs. And I admit that I imagined turning, turning past that dying light and climbing the stairs to see what creaked up there. I imagined opening a door and seeing a wet bed with well-squeezed toys by the pillow and a shape that moved under the covers. One leg of the bed stuck into the floor awkwardly. Creak, creak. And the shape was too small to be there, like that. Much too small.
